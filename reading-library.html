Reading Library

Read quietly, think carefully, then open the answers to check your understanding. There is no speaking task.

20–35 minutes eachGuided A2 · Core B1Gist · detail · interpretation

How to use this library

Three reads, then check.

First: read for the main idea. Second: look for exact details. Third: make an interpretation and support it with words from the text. Open each answer only after you have tried.

Em português: Leia primeiro para entender a ideia geral. Depois procure detalhes. Por fim, pense no que o texto pode significar e use palavras do texto para apoiar sua ideia.

Poem 1 · Christina Rossetti

Who Has Seen the Wind?

Who has seen the wind?
Neither I nor you:
But when the leaves hang trembling,
The wind is passing through.

Who has seen the wind?
Neither you nor I:
But when the trees bow down their heads,
The wind is passing by.

Public-domain poem by Christina Rossetti (d. 1894).

1. Read for gist

What is the poem mainly about?

Show answer and why

Answer: It is about noticing the wind even though we cannot see it. The leaves tremble and the trees bow, so we know the wind is there.

2. Read for detail

What two things do the wind’s effects make us see?

Show answer

Answer: The leaves hang trembling, and the trees bow down their heads.

3. Interpret

What idea might the poem give us about things we cannot see?

Show a strong possible answer

One possible answer: The poem may suggest that invisible things can still be real because we can notice their effects. This is supported by the trembling leaves and bowing trees. Other carefully supported ideas can also be valid.

Language to borrow: I think the poem suggests… · We cannot see…, but we can see…

Poem 2 · Alfred, Lord Tennyson

The Eagle

He clasps the crag with crooked hands;
Close to the sun in lonely lands,
Ringed with the azure world, he stands.

The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
He watches from his mountain walls,
And like a thunderbolt he falls.

Public-domain poem by Alfred, Lord Tennyson (d. 1892). Word help: crag = steep rock / rochedo; azure = bright blue / azul intenso; thunderbolt = raio / relâmpago.

1. Read for gist

Which is the best summary: (a) an eagle rests in a nest, (b) an eagle watches high above the sea and then dives, or (c) a storm destroys a mountain?

Show answer and evidence

Answer: (b). The eagle stands high above the sea, watches, and then “falls” like a thunderbolt.

2. Read for detail

Find one word or phrase that shows height, and one that shows fast movement.

Show answer

Height: “Close to the sun,” “mountain walls,” or “sea beneath him.” Fast movement: “like a thunderbolt he falls.”

3. Interpret

Does the eagle seem calm, weak, or powerful? Explain with two details.

Show a model answer

Model answer: The eagle seems powerful. He is high above the sea, where he can watch everything below. Then he falls “like a thunderbolt,” which makes his movement seem sudden and strong.

Poem 3 · William Blake · Stretch

The Tyger: opening stanza

Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
In the forests of the night;
What immortal hand or eye,
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

Public-domain poem by William Blake (d. 1827). Word help: frame = create / criar; fearful here can mean frightening / que causa medo; symmetry = balanced shape / forma equilibrada.

1. Read for gist

Is the speaker mainly (a) giving instructions to a tiger, (b) asking who could create such a tiger, or (c) telling a story about a forest?

Show answer and why

Answer: (b). The speaker asks, “What immortal hand or eye / Could frame…?” The main idea is wonder about the tiger’s creation.

2. Read for detail

Which two words make the tiger seem bright and dangerous?

Show answer

Answer: “burning” suggests brightness, heat, and danger; “fearful” suggests that the tiger may make people afraid.

3. Interpret

Why might the poet use a question instead of saying, “The tiger is beautiful and frightening”?

Show a strong possible answer

One possible answer: The question makes the reader share the speaker’s wonder. It suggests that the tiger is so powerful and unusual that the speaker cannot easily explain it. This is an interpretation, so another answer is possible if it uses the poem’s words.

Poem 4 · William Wordsworth · Core

Daffodils: an opening image

I wandered lonely as a cloud
That floats on high o’er vales and hills,
When all at once I saw a crowd,
A host, of golden daffodils;
Beside the lake, beneath the trees,
Fluttering and dancing in the breeze.

Public-domain extract from “I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud” by William Wordsworth (d. 1850). Word help: vales = valleys / vales; host = a very large group / grande quantidade; fluttering = moving lightly / tremulando.

1. Read for gist

How does the speaker’s experience change in these lines?

Show answer and evidence

Answer: The speaker begins alone, “lonely as a cloud,” then suddenly sees many bright flowers. The image changes from loneliness to movement, colour, and company.

2. Read for detail

Where are the flowers, and which two verbs make them seem alive?

Show answer

Answer: They are beside a lake and beneath trees. “Fluttering” and “dancing” make them seem alive.

3. Interpret

Why might the poet call the daffodils a “crowd” and a “host”?

Show a model answer

Model answer: The poet calls the flowers a “crowd” and a “host” because there are many of them together. These human-group words also make the flowers feel like cheerful company for the lonely speaker.

Story 1 · Oscar Wilde

The Selfish Giant: a short reading

This is an original A2–B1 retelling for this course, based on Oscar Wilde’s public-domain story.

Every afternoon, children played in a beautiful garden after school. When the Giant returned from a long visit, he was angry. “This is my garden,” he said. “No one may play here.” He built a high wall around it.

After that, spring came to the country, but not to the Giant’s garden. Snow covered the grass. The north wind roared between the trees. The birds did not sing because there were no children there.

One morning, the Giant heard a bird singing. He looked outside and saw that the children had found a small hole in the wall. Wherever a child stood, a tree was full of flowers. The Giant saw a little boy who could not climb a tree. The tree bent its branches down to help him, but the boy was crying.

The Giant understood that he had been selfish. He went outside, lifted the little boy into the tree, and broke down the wall. “This is your garden now,” he told the children. From that day, the garden was full of spring.

1. Read for gist

What changes the Giant most?

Show answer and explanation

Answer: He sees the little boy crying and understands that his selfish rule has hurt the children and the garden. He changes when he helps the boy and removes the wall.

2. Read for detail

Why does spring not come to the garden? What happens when the children return?

Show answer

Answer: Spring does not come because the garden has no children playing in it. When they return, flowers appear near them and the birds sing again.

3. Interpret

What might the wall represent? Give one detail.

Show a strong possible answer

One possible answer: The wall may represent the Giant’s selfishness and separation from other people. When he breaks the wall, he also changes his way of thinking and welcomes the children.

Response frame: The Giant changes because… · The wall may represent… · I know this because…

Story 2 · Saki

The Open Window: a short reading

This is an original A2–B1 retelling for this course, based on Saki’s public-domain story. It prepares students for Unit 4’s setting, clues, and twist ending.

Framton Nuttel was visiting the countryside because he hoped quiet life would help his nerves. He did not know many people there. At one house, a polite girl called Vera spoke to him while she waited for her aunt.

Framton noticed a large open window, even though it was October. Vera looked sadly at it. She explained that three years earlier, her aunt’s husband and two brothers had walked through the window to go hunting. They never returned. Vera said her aunt still kept the window open because she believed they would come back.

Framton listened in silence. Then Vera’s aunt entered the room. She talked happily about the men returning from hunting through the open window. Framton felt nervous. A moment later, three men really did walk across the lawn towards the window, carrying hunting equipment. Framton ran out of the house without saying goodbye.

Vera calmly told her aunt that Framton had suddenly run away because he was afraid of dogs. The reader then understands that Vera invented both stories.

1. Read for gist

Why does Framton run away?

Show answer and why

Answer: He believes Vera’s sad story and thinks he is seeing the missing men return. He does not know that the men were never missing and that Vera invented the story.

2. Read for detail

What clues does the writer give before the ending that make Framton—and the reader—feel uneasy?

Show answer

Answer: The strange open window in October, Vera’s sad expression, and the story about men who disappeared all create suspense. The aunt’s casual words about men returning make the situation feel even stranger to Framton.

3. Interpret

Is Vera only clever, or is she unkind? Give a reason. More than one answer is possible.

Show two supported answers

Clever: Vera invents a convincing story and uses details around her, especially the open window. Unkind: She sees that Framton is nervous but makes him more frightened for her own amusement. A strong answer explains the view and uses a detail.
