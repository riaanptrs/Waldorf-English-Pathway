How to use this unit

Start with what you can honestly say.

These practices do not ask you to become a scientist or historian in English. They help you say what you saw, what a source says, what you think it may mean, and what you still cannot know. This is good writing and good thinking.

Português: Primeiro, diga o que você realmente observou ou leu. Depois, explique o que isso pode significar. Não trate uma opinião ou uma suposição como se fosse fato.

31 · Seeing Carefully · 20–40 min

Observation comes before explanation.

Imagine a drawing of a device with a large wheel, a rope, and a raised platform. It is inspired by Renaissance machine sketches. Do not guess the inventor or purpose yet.

What you can observe

“The drawing shows a wheel and a rope.”

What you infer

“It may have been designed to lift something.”

Useful language

Click a phrase for Portuguese help and an example.

Which sentence is an observation, not an inference?

Try it yourself: choose a bicycle pump, scissors, a ramp, a screw, or a kitchen tool. Write two observations, one possible purpose, and one thing you cannot know. Guided frames: I can see… / It has… / This may… / I cannot know…
Need support? Follow these four steps
  1. Name the object.
  2. Write two things you can see.
  3. Use may for one possible purpose.
  4. Finish with: I cannot know … from this object alone.
Challenge: make your inference stronger

Add one reason: This may be used to lift an object because the rope runs over a wheel. Then explain what extra evidence you would need to be more certain.

32 · Fact, Interpretation, or Exaggeration? · 20–40 min

Strong writing does not overstate.

Read claims about Renaissance astronomy carefully. A fact can be checked. An interpretation explains what facts may mean. An exaggeration says more than the evidence supports.

Example

Fact: Galileo used a telescope to observe the sky. Interpretation: His observations challenged some accepted ideas. Exaggeration: Galileo immediately proved everyone else wrong.

Which sentence is the most responsible historical claim?

Choose the best replacement for “Columbus discovered America.”

Careful-language bank: many people… · at the time… · according to the source… · may have… · did not immediately… · later contributed to…
Need support? Use the claim test

Ask: “Can I check this?” If yes, it may be a fact. Ask: “Does it explain a fact carefully?” If yes, it may be an interpretation. If it says everyone, always, first, proved everything, check whether it exaggerates.

Challenge: revise an over-simple claim

Rewrite: “The telescope changed everything at once.” Use helped, later, some, may have, or did not immediately.

33 · From Notes to a Clear Summary · 25–50 min

Turn notes into a connected paragraph.

A summary is not a list. Select the important information, put it in order, and show the connection.

Paragraph map: topic sentence → key event(s) → why it mattered. Linkers: first, later, however, because of this, as a result.
Reveal a model after your first attempt

Nicolaus Copernicus was a Polish astronomer who developed a Sun-centred model of the planetary system. In 1514, he shared a short account of his ideas. Later, he completed a longer work. His model did not end the debate immediately; however, it influenced later astronomers and changed how people studied the sky.

Need support? Build the paragraph one sentence at a time
  1. Copernicus was a Polish astronomer.
  2. In 1514, he shared a short account of his ideas.
  3. Later, he developed a Sun-centred model.
  4. His ideas influenced later astronomers.

Now join the sentences with later, however, and as a result. Add only facts you can support.

Challenge: improve the model

Add one sentence that distinguishes Copernicus’s proposal from later evidence and debate. Avoid saying that one person “proved everything.”

34 · How a Discovery Changed Ideas · 25–50 min

Explain change: before → change → impact.

Choose one: the printing press, a telescope, a navigation tool, a simple machine, or a Renaissance astronomy idea. Explain how it changed what people could do or think.

Before

What was difficult, believed, or available?

Change

What new idea, tool, or observation appeared?

Impact

What changed later? Was it accepted immediately?

Which linker best completes the explanation? “The printing press made copies faster. ______, ideas could travel to more readers.”

Need support? Use this four-sentence frame

Before ____, people ____. Then ____. As a result, ____. However, ____ did not change immediately.

Challenge: add a limitation

Explain one limit. For example: a tool can make an action easier, but it does not solve every problem or reach every person equally.

35 · Experiment in Words · 25–50 min

Report what happened, then explain carefully.

Use a safe observation: compare a toy car on a shallow and a steeper ramp, watch a video of a lever, or observe how a pulley changes the direction of a pull. Do not use fire, sharp tools, chemicals, or electricity for this practice.

Report structure

Question: What happened? Method: What did you do? Observation: What did you see or measure? Possible explanation: What may explain it? Conclusion: What is a fair answer?

Which conclusion is most careful?

Helpful language: First, we placed… / Next, we changed… / We observed… / The object moved… / This may be because… / The result suggests…
Need support? Complete this report plan before writing
  1. Question: What happens when a toy car uses a steeper ramp?
  2. Method: First, we made two ramps. Next, we released the same toy car.
  3. Observation: The car moved down the steeper ramp more quickly.
  4. Explanation: This may be because the slope was greater.
  5. Conclusion: In this test, the steeper ramp produced a faster movement.
Challenge: make the test fairer

Name one thing you would keep the same (for example, the car, surface, or starting point) and one thing you would repeat. This is the language of a fair test.

36 · A Person in Their Time · 25–50 min

Write a person in context, not a hero story.

Choose Leonardo da Vinci, Galileo Galilei, Copernicus, an artist, or another figure connected to your Grade 7 work. A balanced profile explains who they were, the time they lived in, what they did, and why people still remember them.

Useful language: was born… · lived during… · is known for… · also studied… · not all of… · continues to…
Need support? Use this paragraph order
  1. Who was the person?
  2. What was happening in their time?
  3. What did they do or study?
  4. Why are they remembered today?

Model opening: Leonardo da Vinci was an Italian artist and investigator who lived during the Renaissance. He is known for paintings, but he also filled notebooks with studies of anatomy, water, flight, and machines.

Challenge: avoid a hero story

Add one precise limitation or context sentence: Many of his designs were not built during his lifetime. Then explain why that detail matters.

37 · Wish, Wonder, Surprise: Facts and Voice · 20–45 min

Let knowledge meet your own voice.

Look at a geometry construction, a night-sky map, a simple machine, or a Renaissance artwork from your schoolwork. Write one short response for each heading. Your personal response is welcome; one of your three responses must also include a clear fact or observation.

Geometry in English

Use the careful language of a construction: Draw a circle. Mark the centre. Divide the angle into two equal parts. The two lines meet at… This connects English to the exact geometry of circles, angles, and symmetry studied in Grade 7.

I wish…

What would you like to understand, change, or make?

I wonder…

Ask a real question that you could investigate.

I was surprised…

Name something specific you noticed or learned.

Need support? Use these sentence starters

I wish I could understand how ____. I wonder why ____. I was surprised to see that ____. Add one fact: A hexagon has six sides. or A compass can draw a circle with a fixed radius.

Challenge: connect reflection and evidence

Write 80–100 words. Include one observation, one real question, and one sentence explaining why exact language matters in a geometry instruction.

38 · Mini Research Project · 40–55 min

Research with care, then make it your own.

Choose a Grade 7-linked topic: a simple machine in daily life, an astronomer, navigation by stars, a Renaissance artist-scientist, limestone and marble, a geometric form in nature, or a Brazilian scientist connected to one of these themes.

Use two suitable sources.

A suitable source names an author or organisation, gives a date where possible, and helps you check a factual claim. Do not copy full sentences. Read, close the source, make notes, then write in your own English.

Open the project plan
Paragraph 1: introduce the topic. Paragraph 2: explain the important facts or development. Paragraph 3: explain why it matters and what you learned. Stretch: mention one limitation, question, or different perspective.
Need support? Research in six safe steps
  1. Choose one narrow question, not a huge topic.
  2. Find two suitable sources.
  3. Write five short notes in your own words.
  4. Close the sources before drafting.
  5. Use the three-paragraph plan.
  6. Check every fact and list both sources.

Good starter question: How does an inclined plane make moving an object easier?

Challenge: compare, do not just collect

Use two sources to compare one detail or perspective. Tell the reader if a fact is uncertain, debated, or limited by what the source covers.
